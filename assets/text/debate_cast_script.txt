Moderator:
Welcome everyone to today’s debate. Our topic: Is geoeconomics truly the key to regional power in the 21st century?

Scholar A (Realist voice):
"Geoeconomics is fundamentally about relative gains. States don’t just trade for prosperity; they trade to shift the balance of power. Look at Germany and Moldova: yes, they’re interdependent, but Germany dictates the terms. That’s asymmetrical interdependence in action."

Scholar B (Liberal voice):
"But you neglect the absolute gains! Economic interdependence improves efficiency and prosperity. A liberal order, led by a benign hegemon like Britain in the 19th century or the U.S. after WWII, stabilised the world. Trade reduces conflict."

Scholar A:
"Not quite. Even under hegemons, states manoeuvre to avoid dependence. Protection of strategic industries, transport corridors, and financial tools remains central. Friedrich List already warned in 1841: free trade is the ladder the strong kick away after climbing."

Scholar C (Regionalist voice):
"And that’s why regionalism matters. Regions, like the EU or EAEU, create collective bargaining power. Individually, Moldova or Belarus are weak; within a bloc, they can balance against giants like the U.S. or China."

Scholar D (Critical voice):
"But aren’t regions fragile? The EU has internal tensions — Germany dominates economically, Southern Europe resents it, Britain left altogether. Regional solidarity comes at the price of sovereignty. Smaller members often accept pain just to reduce dependence on hegemonic partners."

Scholar A (closing remark):
"Exactly. The game isn’t efficiency or harmony — it’s power. Asymmetrical interdependence allows states to convert economic weight into political leverage. That’s why geoeconomic regions rise and fall: they’re instruments of strategic autonomy and influence."
